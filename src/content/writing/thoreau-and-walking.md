In a world in which a growing population makes the maximization of productivity the priority, skepticism of industrialism and antagonization toward it seems counterintuitive. However, in 1862, American author and philosopher Henry David Thoreau convinces his audience to fear the changes brought by industrialism by first questioning the changing world, and by framing the related social constructs as tedious in opposition to the concept of "walking in nature."

In his opening lines, Thoreau observes that when walking, humans naturally prefer nature locations such as "the fields and woods," and questions the changing circumstances brought forth by industrialism by asking "what would become of us if we walked only in a garden or mall." The substitution of nature for an artificially created location not only highlights the overall theme that industrialism is encroaching on the natural world, but appeals to human psychology as well. Upon reading this, the audience is likely to feel discomfort, moving from nature — a stimulus the author cleverly associates with the pleasant feeling of walking — to places resulting from industrialism, an idea the author paints as the antithesis of nature.

Toward the end of the essay, the author mentions that he "can see civilization … church and state and school, trade and commerce, and manufactures and agriculture." The author adds that he sees these things "from a hill … afar," further illustrating the contrast between nature and industrialism, which is represented through the affairs of men.

Thus, by first establishing a discomfort at separation from nature and then solidifying industrialism as its contrast, the author successfully ingrains and establishes the value of walking in nature.
